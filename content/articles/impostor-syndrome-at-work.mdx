There's a specific kind of tired that comes from doing your job well and still not believing it. You hit the deadline. The feedback is good. Maybe you even get the offer, the promotion, the thing you were working toward. And some part of you is still waiting to be found out. Not for doing anything wrong. Just for being, underneath it all, not as good as everyone seems to think.

That's impostor syndrome, basically. And the weird part is how little it actually has to do with how good you are at your job.

## It doesn't go away when you succeed

You'd think the fix would be obvious. Get better, get more experience, get the promotion, and the doubt fades on its own. It doesn't work like that though. If anything, success can make it louder. Every new title just comes with a new, quieter voice asking how long until they figure out I don't actually know what I'm doing here.

The achievements pile up. The feeling doesn't move. That gap, between what you've actually done and what you believe about yourself, is basically the whole thing.

## It's not really about the work

The work is just where it shows up. Underneath it, it's usually something older. A fear of being ordinary. A habit of tying your worth to what you produce. Maybe a lifetime of learning that being "found out" as not-enough would be the worst possible thing. Work is just the stage where that fear finally gets an audience: a manager, a team, a deliverable with your name on it.

That's why it doesn't matter how good the results actually are. The fear was never really about the results in the first place.

## The specific ways it shows up

I think most people who deal with this will recognize a few of these:

- Attributing everything to luck. The project went well because the timing worked out, or the team carried it, or the client was easy. Never because you did something right.
- Over-preparing for things you're already qualified for. Re-reading the same email five times before sending it. Rehearsing a meeting you've run a hundred times.
- Feeling weird about praise. A compliment lands less like recognition and more like pressure, because now you have to keep proving it was deserved.
- Being scared of the one question you can't answer. Not because you don't know your job, but because not knowing that one thing feels like it might unravel everything else.
- Comparing your behind-the-scenes to everyone else's highlight reel, and quietly assuming you're the only one who's actually guessing half the time.

## What tends to make it worse

Workplaces that reward constant output don't help. Neither does watching everyone around you perform confidence they probably don't fully feel, because it just teaches you that uncertainty is something to hide rather than something normal. And changing roles, switching industries, or being early in your career makes it worse too, because you genuinely do know less than the people around you in that moment, and your brain has a hard time telling the difference between learning and failing.

## What actually helps

Honestly, not much of it is about fixing your confidence directly. It's usually smaller and slower than that.

Saying it out loud to one person you trust tends to matter more than any mindset trick, because impostor syndrome runs on the belief that you're the only one who feels this way, and that belief rarely survives an honest conversation. Keeping a quiet, private record of things you got right helps more than it sounds like it should, not for anyone else, just for the days when the doubt gets loud. And it helps to separate the fear from the evidence. The fear says you don't belong here. The evidence, if you actually sit down and look at it, usually says something a lot less dramatic. That you're doing something hard, and it's going okay.

## The part nobody really tells you

Impostor syndrome doesn't mean you're an impostor. If anything, the people who feel it most are often the ones taking the work seriously enough to worry about doing it badly. The people who never doubt themselves are rarely the most careful or thoughtful. They're just the loudest about not thinking about it.

You're allowed to be good at something and still be learning it. You're allowed to have earned your place and still feel unsure you deserve it. Both of those can be true at the same time. Neither one is proof that you don't belong.
